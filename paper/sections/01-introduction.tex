\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Generative systems such as Suno and Udio synthesise complete songs, with vocals,
instrumentation, lyrics and arrangement, that listeners struggle to
distinguish from professionally produced human music. Streaming platforms now
report that a substantial fraction of newly uploaded tracks are machine
generated, which turns detection from an academic question into an operational
requirement for distributors, rights organisations and digital service
providers.

Detection is also a forensic problem, and it inherits the asymmetry of one. A
missed synthetic track costs a platform little. A human artist wrongly flagged
as synthetic may lose income or reputation on the strength of a model's
confidence. Any detector proposed for this setting therefore has to be
characterised not only by how well it separates classes in the conditions it was
trained on, but by how it fails when those conditions change, and by whether the
confidence it reports can be trusted at the operating point where the decision
is actually made.

The literature reports a single headline failure: cross-generator collapse.
Cros Vila et al.~\cite{crosvila2025} benchmark full-song detectors under an
explicit cross-generator protocol and observe $F_1$ falling from 0.99
in-distribution to 0.629 on an unseen generative family. That number is widely
cited as the measure of the generalisation problem. We argue that it conflates
two distinct failures, and that separating them changes what the problem is.

Our own results made the case for separating them. Withholding an entire
generator and testing on another cost us almost nothing. The $F_1$ degradation
of 0.037 and 0.023 is an order of magnitude better than the published collapse,
which reads as evidence that a frozen music foundation model generalises across
generators. It is not. The two generators in that experiment, Suno and Udio, are
commercial end-to-end song systems of closely similar design, and the real music
was held fixed only in the sense of coming from one corpus. When we construct
the comparison properly, holding the real recordings literally constant and
substituting generators of genuinely different architecture, the same detector
falls to chance.

This paper makes four contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item We identify a confound in the standard cross-generator protocol. When a
        detector is tested on a new generator, the corpus of \emph{real} music
        usually changes with it, so a failure cannot be attributed to either.
        We separate the two by holding one real corpus fixed across the
        comparison.
  \item Under that control, cross-generator detection fails. A detector trained
        against Suno and Udio falls from AUROC 0.996 to 0.531, chance,
        against five unseen text-to-music models, while its false-positive
        rate on the \emph{same} real recordings does not move (0.069 on both
        sides). It has not stopped recognising real music; it has stopped
        recognising generation. Against AudioLDM2 it is worse than useless
        (AUROC 0.277), ranking generated audio as more authentic than human
        recordings.
  \item We show that apparent cross-generator success is an artefact of
        generator similarity. Suno$\rightarrow$Udio transfer degrades $F_1$ by
        only 0.037, but Suno and Udio are near-identical commercial systems.
        Reported without a cross-architecture comparison, such a number invites
        exactly the wrong conclusion.
  \item We separate this loss of discrimination from a distinct threshold
        failure, in which changing the corpus of real music leaves ranking
        intact but drives the false-positive rate to 1.00, and give two cheap
        diagnostics that expose both: a shortcut test on global descriptors, and
        reporting the all-positive baseline beside every $F_1$.
\end{enumerate}

We also find that the $F_1$ conventionally reported in this setting can be
actively misleading: on our cross-corpus evaluation the detector's $F_1$ of
0.750 is identical, to four decimal places, to that of a classifier that labels
every input synthetic. Reporting $F_1$ alone would have presented a total
failure as an improvement over the published baseline.

The broader methodological point is that a cross-generator number is only as
informative as the distance between the generators, and that distance is almost
never reported. A protocol that does not state it, and does not hold the real
class fixed, cannot distinguish a detector that has learned to identify
synthesis from one that has learned to identify a particular synthesiser.
