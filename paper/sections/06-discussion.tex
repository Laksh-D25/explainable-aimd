\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the detector learned}

The detector learned to recognise Suno and Udio. It did not learn to recognise
generation.

That reading is forced by the false-positive rate. Across the shared-real
evaluation the real class is the same 58 recordings throughout, and the rate at
which they are misclassified is identical, 0.069, whether the synthetic class
is the generator family the detector trained on or five architectures it has
never encountered. Its model of real music is intact and unchanged. What
disappears is discrimination: AUROC 0.996 to 0.531, with $F_1$ of 0.138 against
an all-positive baseline of 0.928, meaning almost every generated clip is
called real.

AudioLDM2 makes the failure mode concrete. At AUROC 0.277 the detector is not
merely uninformative about it; it is inverted, ranking AudioLDM2 output as more
authentic than human recordings with enough consistency to be worse than
guessing. A detector can be wrong in a way that a coin is not.

This also explains the Suno/Udio result rather than contradicting it. Transfer
between two commercial end-to-end song systems of similar design is transfer
between near neighbours. The 0.037 degradation is real, and it is not evidence
of generator-independence, because the experiment never placed any architectural
distance between train and test. Our own paper would have claimed otherwise had
we stopped at that experiment.

\subsection{Two failures, not one}

The cross-corpus and shared-real results isolate different things, and the
contrast between them is the methodological payload.

Changing the corpus of real music drove the false-positive rate to 1.00 while
AUROC fell to 0.598. Holding the corpus fixed and changing the generator drove
AUROC to 0.531 with the false-positive rate unmoved. The discrimination loss
appears in both; the false-positive explosion appears only when the corpus
moves. They are separable, they have different causes, and they call for
different remedies. More diverse generators in training addresses the first;
threshold and calibration work addresses the second.

Reporting either alone misdescribes the system. A study that reports only AUROC
records the FakeMusicCaps result as a moderate degradation and misses that every
genuine artist in the evaluation was flagged. A study that reports only $F_1$ or
accuracy at a fixed threshold, as most in this literature do, cannot separate
them at all.

\subsection{What this means for deployment}

A detector validated on cross-generator splits alone may be validated on nothing.
If the held-out generator is architecturally close to the trained ones, as it
usually is between commercial song generators, the resulting number carries
no information about the case that matters, which is the generator that did not
exist when the detector was trained.

We would therefore argue that cross-generator evaluation should report the
architectural distance it spans, and that a detector intended for forensic use
should be tested against at least one generator of a different family, with the
real class held fixed so the result is attributable. Neither is expensive. Both
would have prevented the conclusion we nearly drew.

\subsection{Why $F_1$ is the wrong headline}

Our cross-corpus $F_1$ of 0.750 exceeds the published cross-generator baseline of
0.629, and means nothing. It is exactly the score of a classifier that labels
everything synthetic. The metric is not defective; it is being asked to do
something it cannot, which is to distinguish a detector from a constant
function when classes are unbalanced.

Two cheap conventions prevent this. Report the all-positive baseline beside every
$F_1$, and report false-positive rate alongside it. Either would have caught our
degenerate result immediately. We would encourage their adoption as a norm in
this literature, where class balance varies widely between evaluation sets and
the cost of a false positive is the main reason the work is being done.

\subsection{Calibration as a first-class result}

The robustness table shows AUROC holding above 0.989 under four of six
degradations while $F_1$ falls by up to 0.233, and the cross-corpus evaluation
shows calibration error rising from $1.5\times10^{-4}$ to 0.365. In both cases
the ranking survives and the decision rule does not.

The mechanism is visible in threshold selection. Where validation separates the
classes perfectly, the usual false-positive-budget threshold lands against the
real class and any distribution shift sweeps the entire real population across
it. Placing the threshold at the midpoint of the separating margin is a one-line
change that moved false-positive rate from 1.00 to 0.00 in our cross-generator
evaluation. Detectors intended for forensic use should report the threshold rule
as part of the method, not as an implementation detail.

\subsection{Explanation, and what it is evidence of}

The faithfulness asymmetry, in which every explanation of real audio beats its
random baseline and none of generated audio does, is a negative result about the
method, not about the model. It says that whatever the detector uses to identify
synthesis is not concentrated in time, and therefore that temporal attribution,
which dominates the audio-explainability literature, cannot surface it.

That is consistent with the frequency-domain characterisation of AI-music
artefacts~\cite{afchar2025ismir}, and it is why we separated the frequency claim
from the temporal one in Section~\ref{sec:method} rather than presenting a
single Grad-CAM-style saliency map over a spectrogram the model never sees.

The layer-level comparison carries a second warning. An attention-pooling
architecture hands the analyst a ready-made explanation, the softmax over
layers, and it is tempting to report it, because it costs nothing and looks
like an answer. In our model it is flat to within 1.09$\times$ and its ordering
is negatively rank-correlated with the exact Shapley attribution over the same
layers in every audited song. The attention weights are not a weak version of
the Shapley values; they point the other way. Architectural attention is
evidence about the architecture, not about the decision, and papers that report
it as an explanation should be read accordingly. This is the concrete payoff of
running Levels~1 and~3 as a contrast rather than as two independent views: the
disagreement is the finding.
