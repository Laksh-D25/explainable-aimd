\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A detector built on a frozen music foundation model transfers almost perfectly
between Suno and Udio, losing 0.037 and 0.023 in $F_1$ where previous work
reported a loss of 0.361. Read on its own, that number says the generalisation
problem is smaller than the literature claims. It says no such thing.

When the corpus of real music is held literally fixed and only the generator
varies, the same detector falls from AUROC 0.996 to 0.531. Chance. The
false-positive rate on those same recordings does not move, staying at 0.069
across every comparison, so the model of real music survives intact while the
ability to identify generation does not. Against AudioLDM2 the detector is
inverted rather than merely uninformed, scoring 0.277 and ranking synthetic
audio above human recordings.

Suno and Udio transfer to one another because they are near neighbours,
commercial end-to-end song systems built along similar lines. Put architectural
distance between training and test and the transfer disappears.

A cross-generator result is only as informative as the distance it spans. That
distance is almost never stated.

We also separate two failures that the literature reports as one. Changing the
corpus of real music drives the false-positive rate to 1.00 while leaving
ranking largely intact; changing the generator destroys ranking while leaving
the false-positive rate untouched. Different causes, different remedies, and
neither visible in an $F_1$ reported at a fixed threshold. Our own cross-corpus
$F_1$ of 0.750 matches, to four decimal places, a classifier that labels every
input synthetic.

Two conventions would have caught each of these, and we recommend both. Report
the all-positive baseline beside every $F_1$, and report the false-positive rate
on real music alongside AUROC. To those we add a third: state the architectural
distance a cross-generator evaluation spans, and hold the real class fixed
across it. All three are cheap. Without them, a detector can be validated on
nothing and appear to work.

Ablations show the architecture is not the source of the in-distribution
accuracy: discarding twelve of thirteen hidden states, or replacing attention
pooling with a mean, leaves every metric unchanged. Explanation audits show
gradient-based temporal attribution is faithful for real audio and unfaithful
for generated audio, and that the model's own attention weights are negatively
rank-correlated with exact Shapley attribution over the same layers. The case
for this architecture rests on the explanations it makes available, not on its
accuracy.
